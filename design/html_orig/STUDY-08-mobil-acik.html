Urban Hierarchy in Indus Valley

312 Kelime · C1 Akademik Düzey

Although initial archaeological excavations indicated a strictly egalitarian social structure devoid of monumental tombs, subsequent stratigraphical analyses have revealed profound material disparities across Harappan urban sectors. Standardized brick dimensions, centralized drainage systems, and uniform weights once led scholars to hypothesize a cooperative society devoid of coercive class divisions.

However, detailed mapping of residential units at Mohenjo-daro indicates that domestic quarters varied drastically in domestic space, resource allocation, and access to imported luxury artifacts such as lapis lazuli and carnelian beads. The pronounced residential segregation implies that a distinct administrative oligarchy exercised subtle yet rigid bureaucratic oversight over artisanal guilds and agrarian yields.

Consequently, modern anthropology increasingly views the Indus phenomenon not as a primordial socialist commonwealth, but as a meticulously calibrated hierarchy where civic stability was sustained through institutional conformity and economic specialization rather than ostentatious militaristic intimidation.
