Line 1

For decades, prevailing archaeological models characterized the mature urban phase of the Indus Valley Civilization as an exceptionally uniform and structurally egalitarian society. Unlike their contemporaries in Mesopotamia and Pharaonic Egypt, the inhabitants of major settlements like Mohenjo-daro and Harappa left behind no monumental royal tombs, dynastic palaces, or ostentatious victory stelae commemorating individual autocrats.

Line 5

However, rigorous re-evaluations of micro-stratigraphical sequences across periphery districts have challenged this consensus. Systematic dental morphometrics and strontium isotope ratios derived from cemetery populations reveal stark disparities in nutritional access, biological stress indicators, and physical trauma rates between intramural residential compounds.

Line 10

These localized discrepancies indicate that the absence of overt administrative iconography did not signify an absence of institutionalized socioeconomic hierarchy. Rather, corporate governance models likely distributed authority among non-kin guild factions whose power was negotiated through standardized metrology, trade access, and sanitary sanitation control rather than through militaristic coercion.

Line 15

Consequently, the Indus societal matrix emerges not as a utopian collective, but as an intricate civic apparatus where structural dominance was codified quietly within everyday urban zoning and technical standardization.
